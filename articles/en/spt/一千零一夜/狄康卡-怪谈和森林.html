Dikonka - Ghost Stories and the Forest

Did you know? Liars will turn into big trees, monsters in the forest, red sunsets, and sand sinking at the bottom of rivers.

So, don't walk into that unknown, monster-filled forest, don't stare directly at the sun, don't swallow the silt from the river—maybe you're swallowing a person.

Liars must swallow a thousand needles—no, no, no, how can a child's joke be trusted? But the story I'm about to tell is actually a real and believable one. Please believe me, those who break promises will be punished. Of course, evil is punished, but good deeds also come at a price. Everything is an equivalent exchange; there's no such thing as getting everything without giving.

Why do I say this? Please keep listening.

A long, long time ago, in a small village, the atmosphere was simple and simple. The people were very warm, and every household knew each other. Children were out running, playing, and playing games every day. But the adults always warned them that they couldn't go into the forest, because monsters would eat you—because once you go, you can never come back! Because there's no turning back!

Children are always curious about this, and some ask adults, 'What's really in the forest? Why can't we all go in?' But why can adults go in? Why are there always guards ahead of the forest, not letting us children go through? Why do some people go in and never come back? Why do some people go in and leave unharmed?

But the adults always roughly push away the children with many questions, speaking in somewhat rude tones, saying, 'Go on, go on, every child has so many questions. There aren't so many whys, nor so many answers. If you can't go, you can't go. Just remember this.'

So the children stopped asking and behaved themselves. But even though they stopped asking, these questions seemed to take root in their hearts. At night, as they went to sleep, they would always wonder what was in the forest, and why the adults wouldn't let them go. In the morning, while playing, the children would gather together to chat secretly, making up stories about the monsters in the woods.

Some children said it was a terrifying black beast with fangs that kept bleeding, but they feared the light and hid in the shadowy woods, refusing to come out. But once someone passed by, they would immediately devour the child!

Some children said they were actually monsters that looked human-like, but their skin was pale and they looked more like monsters than humans. However, they would disguise themselves as humans to make themselves look less like monsters, then quietly approach people, luring them into their lairs to eat them. They didn't dare leave the forest because if they went out and were discovered, they would be driven away.

Some children said there were no monsters anywhere; it was just that the forest was full of poisonous creatures, snakes, wolves, tigers, and leopards. That's why the adults wouldn't let them in, even making up stories about monsters to scare them.

Some children, trembling and cautious, said that adults don't like them discussing these things, so it's better to stop talking.

So the children, still wanting more, stopped and continued the ball throwing game, only returning home at dusk. There were many opinions, no definite answer, but the more sternly the adults warned, the more curious the children became. Until one day, while the children were playing the usual ball tossing and catching game, a little girl missed catching the ball and it ran far away. She hurriedly chased after it, but the ball rolled into the deep forest. It happened to be shift time, and there was briefly no guard ahead of the forest. The girl gritted her teeth and ran into the woods. Naturally, her heart was afraid. She didn't know what lay ahead, what monsters might exist, or whether she might disappear—if she could, maybe she wouldn't have entered this forest either.

But the ball rolling far away was only a fuse; she had long wanted to go into the forest. This rolling ball merely made her make up her mind, take a step, and walk into the forest. She had long wanted to know what kind of monsters were really in the forest, and why the adults kept warning her so insistently.

So she swallowed her saliva and then resolutely stepped forward, walking into the forest.

Unexpectedly, the forest was actually very quiet. There weren’t the deep red, overwhelming thorns and leaves that children imagined, nor were there the black, man-eating monsters that adults spoke of. Curious, the girl walked forward, and somehow the trees naturally parted to make way for her. In front of her spread a path that was slightly winding but led straight ahead.

She broke branches around her as markers, and as she stepped on the leaves, they made hoarse, squeaky sounds. She quickened her pace, and the squeaky sound grew louder, until the light ahead grew brighter and brighter, until she was no longer in a canopy-covered forest but in an open meadow. What she saw was no longer branches blocking her sight, replaced instead by rivers; what she heard was the melodious chirping of birds, the rustling of green leaves, the fluttering of white doves, and flowers sparsely blooming on the grass.

—This shouldn’t be a forbidden place, it shouldn’t be a land where one dies without a grave; perhaps it should be an Eden instead. Suddenly, this thought occurred to the girl.

As for the so-called land where one dies without a grave, it was a name the adults gave based on the occasional deaths that happened in this forest.

But what the girl saw was not a monster at all; she saw a different kind of creature, one could say it overturned her understanding—

She saw it—what did she see?

…… She saw the woman sitting on the grass, smiling at the children running from afar. She saw the boy swaying back and forth from the branch with two vines on the branch; she saw the curious child squatting by the river, watching his reflection in the clear water, combing his hair; she saw the child covering himself and playing hide-and-seek with other children; besides that, she saw other women sitting under another tree, chatting idly. And then...... And then, the woman washing clothes in the river, the woman weaving garlands, the woman caressing children, the woman singing lullabies. And without exception, all those women had pointed ears...... But the children did not, just like the other children in the village—they still had round ears.

The girl covered her mouth in shock and took two steps back in fear. Everything before her was indeed very warm, even stirring a genuine sense of longing. But she didn't even know where this attachment came from, and it made her fearful. She felt that something that would break her common sense was about to happen.

Coincidentally, as she stepped back, she stepped on scattered branches, making a creaking sound. Everyone noticed and looked toward the girl.

One woman widened her eyes and anxiously stood up, ready to run toward the girl. She moved quickly, accidentally stepping on an overly long skirt and nearly falling. She simply ran toward the girl without hesitation, while the other women watched the scene in front of them with friendly eyes.

She knelt on both knees, falling before the girl. Her hands trembled, her lips trembling. After hesitating for a long while, she reached out to hug the girl, perhaps afraid it was all just an illusion. At first, the woman's strength was light, as if it would disappear in the next moment. Then she tightened her grip and held the girl tightly, making her feel a bit of pain.

But she felt a different kind of warmth from it, completely different from the embrace of those in the village who claimed to be their father—yes, self-proclaimed. Now the girl fully understood this. You could say she was self-taught, realizing it was just self-title—absurd and laughable.

So she couldn't help but reach out and hug the woman back.

The woman said, 'Child, you are the child I have searched for for so long, and today I finally found it...... You are the child I have lost for so long.

The girl said, "Mom, long time no see, I miss you so much."

Yes, for some reason, she was already in tears. She realized the word, and although she didn't know what it meant, she knew it was meant to address the woman in front of her.

The woman smiled, "I miss you too, child." It was all those annoying men who took our child away...... And we were powerless. Fortunately, I can still see my child; thankfully, before I die, I can still see you.

The girl sobbed and asked, "Why is that?" What exactly happened? Why did those people take these children? What are they trying to do?

After all, she was just a child who knew nothing. She desperately wanted an answer, even if it was something she didn't want to know. Even though the truth might be cruel, she had to know. At this moment, she seemed to have grown up, as if she was no longer the child she once was.

Another woman in a green robe approached, also kneeling on her knees, slowly telling a story to the two who were sobbing uncontrollably.

For some reason, men and women lived separately. Maybe they lived together long ago, but later they were customarily separated, and those men no longer disturbed life in the woods, that is, in the Garden of Eden.

Conversely, they also met one condition. Every October, the woman would provide five children to the outside world. From then on, there would be no chance to meet again, because these women would die after raising two children, so they could no longer see their children and could only enjoy some tender moments before the exchange.

Girls who grew up outside would return to Eden, never getting another chance to leave, while boys raised outside would still stay there. Conversely, as a trade-off, women required men to fulfill five promises each year.

Some women are kind-hearted, only asking to bring her a flower. Some are pragmatic, asking to be built a wooden house to bring food from outside. Some women deliberately make things difficult, asking the man to pluck the moon from the horizon and give it to her.

Because only when promises are not fulfilled do women have ways to torment these men. They don't blame those who make simple demands, after all, they are not their enemies.

As for the men who failed to keep their promises, some screamed in terror as they were devoured by monsters disguised as women; some turned back into matter, becoming sand and silt at the bottom of the river; others looked exhausted, their bodies stiff and stiff, then turned into identical trees. Others rolled on the ground in pain, as if a thousand silver needles were stabbing their throats, and finally died on the ground, dying on the ground.

This is the truth that people in the forest will go missing and die.

After all...... Breaking promises comes at a price, and of course, doing bad things also comes with a price. Their experience at this moment is the cost, and the promises they had to fulfill to steal those five children were also the price.

…… You want to ask me what the price of doing good deeds is? Then please listen to the rest of the story.

The girl learned the truth of the story and decided to change everything. She didn't want to see her own people suffer again, and she didn't want such things to happen again. But the women advised her not to keep trying; maintaining the status quo was fine, and perhaps it was fine. As they said this, their faces showed unwillingness and frustration.

So the girl's anger—perhaps because she was young, still had the courage to move forward, and the determination to change everything. She angrily asked, Is maintaining the status quo really good? Is it good to remain a victim? Is it really good to have anger and fear in your heart?

The women looked at each other, and finally, the first to kneel and embrace her was her mother, saying she was willing to try again. More and more women joined in, some indignant—some picked up stones, others picked up branches to use as protective weapons, even though that might be nothing.

Then, for the first time, they left the forest together, chased away the guards at the entrance, defeated the men who treated them like monsters, and became the rulers of the village. So the remaining children learned that there were no monsters in the forest; they were stories made up by the adults, or rather, men.

Yet isn't all of this just a beautiful fairy tale? The girl, or rather the boy, raised her arms and shouted, hoping more people would join her, but those people had long since become numb and lost the courage to struggle; only a few were willing to follow. She carried the truth and led those women out of the forest, but most children did not believe her words, and the adults only treated her and other women who came out of the forest as monsters and monsters, attacking them. After struggling to resist and speak out, they ultimately fell into despair, and all that awaited them was death.

This is the price—the price of doing good, using life as the foundation of justice, freedom, courage, and kindness.

A long, long time ago, people lived together in this Garden of Eden, regardless of gender, without distinction, simply running and laughing freely among the shade of trees and the babbling stream, enjoying a happy life. After all, virtue is happiness, and joy is the supreme pleasure.

But one day, things changed. A man—at least as we call it now—was a man. They had tasted the forbidden fruit, and they probably knew something, so they put on their clothes and left Eden in a proper manner. And the women remained in the Garden of Eden, not knowing what had happened, but because the words of those men had also put on clothes.

Yet they continued to freely enjoy unrestrained happiness in the Garden of Eden. But one day, everything changed. Those men once again entered Eden—because after leaving, they were no longer welcomed. The men who entered forced the remaining women in Eden to have sex—in other words, to have intercourse. The women wept, beat them, and begged, but found no release, only receiving even more violent assaults.

Afterwards, the men left again. Behind were women left in disarray and devoid of any desire to live.

But as their bellies grew larger and painful, happiness also emerged. They felt love and sincere affection for their unborn child, and this rekindled their hope to survive.

Unfortunately, the good times didn't last—after that, after ten months of painful pregnancy, the women held their children in tears, loving them, but then the men broke in again, took the children, and assaulted them once more. Compared to a real person, or rather, it was just tools and machines.

So, they could only painfully continue this process, repeating the cycle and hating those men. But without weapons, they could do nothing. Those girls who were taken away would return to Eden when they grew up, becoming tools themselves; the boys who were taken would grow up carefree in the outside world, continuing all this evil.

—This is the truth of the story.
